Chapter 14 · The last mile

cuBLAS, the last 6%, and the whole ladder in one view

Ten kernels in, we are at 93.7% of a library that has had two decades of engineers on it. The remaining gap is instructive, because it is not one clever trick. Then the whole climb on one chart, with the three regimes marked.

Reference · cuBLAS · 23,250 GFLOP/s · 100% by definition · 78% of the 30 TFLOP/s the chip can do

Kernel 10 is one file, and on the 4,092 × 4,092 problem it runs within about six percent of cuBLAS. It's worth being clear about what that six percent is, because the temptation is to imagine one more optimization that closes it. There isn't one. The gap is three structural things a library does that a single kernel doesn't, and this chapter animates each of them. Then it puts all ten kernels back on the roofline from chapter 2 and on one ladder, so the whole argument of the book can be read off a single picture.

14.1cuBLAS is not one kernel

Boehm profiled what cuBLAS actually launches for a square multiply at different sizes, and the answer changes with the size. At 128 and 512 it runs a kernel with 32 × 32 tiles. At 256 it runs a 64 × 32 kernel and then a second, separate kernel called a split-K reduce. From 1,024 upward it switches to a 128 × 64 kernel. On Ampere the library carries around sixteen different SGEMM kernels and picks between them at runtime based on the matrix shapes. Our kernel 10 is tuned for one shape; cuBLAS carries a tuned kernel for practically every shape, which is chapter 12's autotuning taken to its logical conclusion.

Why smaller tiles for smaller problems? Because a tile is also a block, and a block runs on one SM. With 128 × 128 tiles, a 128 × 128 multiply is a single block: one SM working, eighty-three idle. The animation counts how many of the 84 SMs each problem size can keep busy, and it is the reason our kernel 10 is competitive at 2,048 and 4,096, within 1–6% of cuBLAS, and far behind at 128 to 512.

14.2It changes strategy for awkward shapes: split-K

When the matrices are small or thin there is not enough independent work to fill 84 SMs, however you tile the output. cuBLAS then splits the k dimension instead: several blocks each compute a partial sum over their own slice of k for the same tile of C, and a second pass adds the partials. More total work, more memory traffic, and a better-filled machine. For the 256 × 256 case that second pass is the splitKreduce_kernel in the table.

14.3It overlaps the loads with the arithmetic

Our kernels have a rhythm that has not changed since kernel 3: load a chunk into shared memory, barrier, compute on it, barrier, load the next chunk. While the block is loading, its lanes are idle; while it is computing, the memory system is idle. cuBLAS prefetches. It keeps two chunks' worth of shared memory, and while computing on chunk n it is already fetching chunk n + 1 into the other buffer, so the arithmetic units rarely stop. This is called double buffering, and it is the main structural idea the ten kernels skipped.

14.4And even cuBLAS is at 78%

cuBLAS reaches 23,250 GFLOP/s on this problem, and the chip's fp32 lanes could do 30,000. The last fifth goes to things no amount of software removes: the k-loop's own bookkeeping instructions, the barriers, the tails at the matrix edges, the gaps between one block finishing and the next starting, and the fact that a real instruction stream cannot keep every lane fed on every cycle. The one way past that ceiling is to stop doing fp32 arithmetic on the lanes at all and use the tensor cores, which is a different instruction set, a different precision, and a different book.

14.5The roofline, revisited

Chapter 2 promised to come back to the roofline with all ten kernels on it. Here they are, with one caveat that matters. The intensity plotted is the bytes each kernel's SMs request from the memory system per FLOP, which is what the kernel controls; the L2 cache serves many of those requests, so DRAM sees fewer bytes than the axis says. That is why kernels 1 and 2 float above the DRAM roof: their re-reads mostly hit L2. From kernel 3 on, the dots sit under the DRAM roof and climb toward the compute roof, which is the picture of a kernel limited by instructions, not by bytes.

Reading down the third column, the same idea appears in different clothes each time. Kernel 2 is about the shape of a request. Kernel 3 is about not asking twice. Kernels 4 and 5 are about getting more arithmetic out of each thing you asked for. Kernel 6 is about asking in fewer instructions. Kernels 9 and 10 are about fitting the request to the specific machine. Underneath all of them is the sentence from chapter 2.

The one sentence, one last time

Every kernel in this book does the same 137 GFLOP of arithmetic. What changed, every single time, was how many bytes had to move to feed it, and how many instructions were spent moving them. Performance is the ratio between arithmetic and traffic, and each rewrite raised that ratio.
