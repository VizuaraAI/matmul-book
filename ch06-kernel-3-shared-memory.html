Chapter 6 · The memory-starved kernels

Kernel 3: caching a tile in shared memory

Inside one block, every number is fetched thirty-two times. This kernel fetches it once, into a scratchpad the whole block can read. We watch it run, every load and every multiply, on a version small enough to see all of.

Kernel · sgemm_shared_mem_block · 2,980 GFLOP/s · 12.8% of cuBLAS · measured by Simon Boehm on an NVIDIA A6000

Kernel 2 fixed the shape of every request and left the number of requests exactly where kernel 1 had it: 137 billion loads, 548 GB asked for. Chapter 5 ended by naming the two kinds of redundancy hiding behind that number. Between blocks, all 128 blocks along a row of tiles read the same row strip of A. Inside a block, the thread that owns C[0][0] and the thread that owns C[0][1] both walk the whole of row 0 of A, and each of them fetches it separately.

This chapter is about the second one. The fix is short (nine lines change) but it introduces a new kind of memory, a new kind of instruction, and a loop with a different shape, and all three are easier to see than to describe. So this chapter is built around a version of the kernel that is small enough to draw completely.

6.1A version small enough to see all of

Everything in kernel 3 comes in units of one number, BLOCKSIZE. The block computes a BLOCKSIZE × BLOCKSIZE tile of C, so it has BLOCKSIZE² threads. It walks the k dimension in chunks of BLOCKSIZE. The two tiles it keeps in shared memory are BLOCKSIZE × BLOCKSIZE each. Boehm's kernel uses 32, and 32 × 32 = 1,024 threads over 4,092 values of k is far too much to draw a cell at a time.

So for this chapter, set BLOCKSIZE to 4 and K to 8. The block is now 16 threads computing a 4 × 4 tile of C; it needs a 4 × 8 slab of A and an 8 × 4 slab of B; k breaks into two chunks of four; and the shared-memory tiles are 4 × 4. Nothing else changes. It is the same code, the same one-thread-per-output, the same rule that each thread copies exactly one element of A and one of B per chunk. The numbers are the top-left corner of the 8 × 8 example the book has used since chapter 4, so every product you see is a real one.

6.2The problem, seen whole

First, kernel 2 on the miniature, so the thing we are fixing is in plain view. Sixteen threads, one per cell of C. Thread (1, 2) owns C[1][2], so it needs row 1 of A and column 2 of B, and it walks them with the loop from chapter 5: at each k it loads one value of A and one of B from global memory, multiplies, and adds into tmp. Eight steps, sixteen loads, all of them trips to DRAM.

Now look at the three other threads in row 1 of the tile. They own C[1][0], C[1][1] and C[1][3], so they walk the same row 1 of A, and each of them loads every one of its values on its own. Four threads, four separate trips for the same number, and the same again for every column of B.

The count at the end of the animation is the whole problem. The block needs 4 × 8 + 8 × 4 = 64 distinct numbers and it issues 16 × 8 × 2 = 256 loads: every number, four times, because a tile with four columns has four threads walking each row of A, and a tile with four rows has four threads walking each column of B. At full size the tile has 32 of each, the block needs 2 × 32 × 4,092 = 261,888 numbers, and it issues 1,024 × 4,092 × 2 = 8,380,416 loads. Thirty-two times over.

6.3The idea: fetch once, read four times

The fix writes itself once the picture is in front of you. Fetch each value from global memory once, put it somewhere all four threads can reach, and let them read it from there.

A register won't do. A register belongs to one thread and no other thread can see it, which is the same property that made it free to read. Shared memory is the other thing on the SM: a scratchpad of up to 48 KB per block, sitting physically on the chip beside the arithmetic units, visible to every thread of that block. Nothing arrives there by accident; you write the copy yourself, as an ordinary assignment. And once a value is in it, reading it costs on the order of 20 to 30 cycles instead of the several hundred a trip to DRAM costs.

The contrast with a cache is the part to keep. A cache decides for you. It holds what you touched recently, by a policy you don't control, and it may or may not still have your value by the time you come back for it, which is exactly why chapter 5 could say the L2 softens a bad access pattern without repairing it. Shared memory decides nothing. It holds what you put there, for as long as you leave it there, and if you want a value in it you have to write the line that copies it. That is more work, and the extra work is the whole reason it can be counted on.

6.4Why we can't copy everything at once

In the miniature the whole slab of A is 32 numbers and the whole slab of B another 32, and 256 bytes would fit in shared memory a hundred times over. At full size it doesn't fit, and it isn't close. A's strip is 32 × 4,092 floats, which is 523,776 bytes, about 512 KB. B's strip is another 512 KB. The budget is 48 KB. We are over by a factor of about 21.

But the loop is an accumulation, and an accumulation doesn't need all of its terms at once. tmp += A[…] * B[…] can eat the k dimension in pieces, as long as the pieces cover it in the end. So chop k into chunks of BLOCKSIZE. One chunk of A is then a 32 × 32 square: 1,024 floats, 4 KB. One chunk of B is another 4 KB. Eight kilobytes for a pair, comfortably inside the 48. And 4,092 ÷ 32 = 127.9, so the k dimension breaks into 128 chunks, the last of them partial. In the miniature the same rule gives two chunks of four.

6.5Kernel 3, every step

Here is Boehm's kernel, with BLOCKSIZE = 32. It is still one thread per output element and the inner arithmetic is still one multiply-add per k step. What's new is the two copies at the top of the loop, the two barriers around the arithmetic, and the fact that the arithmetic reads As and Bs, which live in shared memory, instead of A and B.

// BLOCKSIZE = 32; one 1-D block of 1,024 threads computes the tile at (cRow, cCol)
const uint threadRow = threadIdx.x / BLOCKSIZE;   // 0 … 31
const uint threadCol = threadIdx.x % BLOCKSIZE;   // 0 … 31, consecutive across a warp

__shared__ float As[BLOCKSIZE * BLOCKSIZE];      // 4 KB, lives on the SM
__shared__ float Bs[BLOCKSIZE * BLOCKSIZE];      // 4 KB

A += cRow * BLOCKSIZE * K;                        // this block's row strip of A
B += cCol * BLOCKSIZE;                            // this block's column strip of B
C += cRow * BLOCKSIZE * N + cCol * BLOCKSIZE;

float tmp = 0.0;                                // the running total, in a register
for (int bkIdx = 0; bkIdx < K; bkIdx += BLOCKSIZE) {
    As[threadRow * BLOCKSIZE + threadCol] = A[threadRow * K + threadCol];
    Bs[threadRow * BLOCKSIZE + threadCol] = B[threadRow * N + threadCol];
    __syncthreads();                              // nobody reads until everybody has written

    A += BLOCKSIZE;                               // slide the window along k
    B += BLOCKSIZE * N;

    for (int dotIdx = 0; dotIdx < BLOCKSIZE; ++dotIdx)
        tmp += As[threadRow * BLOCKSIZE + dotIdx] * Bs[dotIdx * BLOCKSIZE + threadCol];

    __syncthreads();                              // nobody overwrites until everybody has read
}
C[threadRow * N + threadCol] = alpha * tmp + beta * C[threadRow * N + threadCol];

The three lines above the loop are bookkeeping: they move each pointer to where this block's work begins, A to the top of its 32-row strip, B to the left edge of its 32-column strip, C to the corner of its tile. Everything after that is written as though the block were the whole problem. threadRow and threadCol run 0 to 31 and mean "inside this tile", which is why the two copies look like they are reading the top-left corner of A and B when they are usually reading somewhere in the middle of a 4,092 × 4,092 matrix. The two bumps inside the loop, A += BLOCKSIZE and B += BLOCKSIZE * N, slide that window along k: 32 columns to the right in A, 32 rows down in B.

Read the loop with the miniature's numbers and it says this. Thread (r, c) is one of sixteen. At the top of each chunk it copies A[r][c] of the current window into As[r][c], and B[r][c] of the current window into Bs[r][c]. One element of each; sixteen threads fill both 4 × 4 tiles. Then it waits at the barrier until all sixteen copies are in. Then it runs the inner loop four times, reading row r of As and column c of Bs, and adds four products into tmp. Then it waits at the second barrier. Then the window slides and it does it again. After the second chunk, tmp holds the whole eight-term dot product, and it writes it to C. The animation below runs exactly that, with the line of code being executed marked as it goes.

Three things to take from the run. The copies are the only lines that touch global memory, and across both chunks the block issued 64 of them, one for each of the 64 distinct values it needed. The inner loop touched only shared memory: 256 reads for the block, every one of them fast, and each As value was read by the four threads of its row, each Bs value by the four threads of its column. And tmp never moved. It sat in its register from the first product to the last, took the second chunk's products on top of the first's, and went to C exactly once.

Nothing about the sharing went away. Row 1 of As is still read by all four threads that own row 1 of the tile; column 2 of Bs is still read by the four threads in column 2. What changed is where they read it from: one slow trip to global memory and then four fast reads out of the scratchpad, instead of four slow trips.

6.6What a barrier is

Two lines in that loop do no arithmetic at all, and the kernel is wrong without either of them.

__syncthreads() makes every thread of the block stop at that line until all of them have reached it. A meeting point, nothing more.

You need it because threads don't run in lockstep. A block's warps are handed to the SM's four schedulers, and a scheduler runs whichever of its warps is ready; a warp waiting on a load steps aside and another one runs in its place. Some warps get a long way ahead. Without the first barrier, a thread that has finished its own copy can walk straight into the inner loop and read As[1][3] before the thread responsible for As[1][3] has written it. What it gets is whatever was sitting in that slot: the previous chunk's value, or uninitialized memory on the first chunk. The answer comes out wrong, sometimes, depending on how the warps happened to be scheduled, which is about the worst kind of wrong there is.

The second barrier prevents the mirror image. Once a fast thread finishes its multiply-adds it wants to start the next chunk's copy, and that copy overwrites As and Bs. If a slow thread is still partway through its inner loop, the values under it change while it is reading them.

Aside · two bugs, one loop apart

Both failures are read-write races on shared memory and they are symmetric: the first barrier stops a read from overtaking a write, the second stops a write from overtaking a read. Both are timing-dependent, so a kernel missing one of them can pass a test and fail in production, or pass on one GPU and fail on the next. The fast threads do pay for the barriers (they stand at the line doing nothing until the last warp turns up) and that waiting is real, measurable time. It buys an answer that is the same every run.

6.7Back to full size

The real kernel is the miniature with every 4 replaced by 32 and the 8 replaced by 4,092. Each thread still copies one element of A and one of B per chunk, and now there are 128 chunks, so the window over row 5 of A slides 32 columns at a time, 128 times, while tmp accumulates a 4,092-term dot product without ever leaving its register.

And the accounting scales the same way. Per chunk the block issues 2,048 global loads (1,024 threads × 2), does 65,536 shared-memory reads (1,024 × 64) and 32,768 multiply-adds. Over 128 chunks that is 262,144 global loads against kernel 2's 8,380,416: the factor of 32 we set out to get, landing almost exactly on the 261,888 distinct values the block needs. Per element of C, kernel 2 spent 2K = 8,184 global loads; kernel 3 spends K/16 = 256.

Key

Shared memory didn't remove the sharing. It moved it: one slow trip to global memory, then 32 fast reads of the scratchpad, in place of 32 slow trips.

Boehm measures 2,980.3 GFLOP/s, up from 1,986.5. That is 1.5×, and it takes the kernel from 8.5% of cuBLAS to 12.8%.

A 32× cut in global traffic buying 1.5× in speed is a large miss, not a small one. If global memory had still been the thing this kernel waited on, cutting its traffic by 32 would have shown up as something close to 32×. Chapter 5 made a prediction of exactly that shape, out of nothing but a sector count, and it landed within 3%. Run the same reasoning here and it is off by a factor of twenty.

The reasoning isn't what broke; the premise underneath it is. Global memory stopped being the limit somewhere between kernel 2 and this one, and the kernel is now waiting on something else. Naming that something is the next chapter's job, and it is the hinge of the book: every kernel from 4 onward is an attack on what chapter 7 finds.
