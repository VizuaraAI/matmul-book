Chapter 1 · Foundations

The problem: one matrix multiply, 137 billion operations

Before any kernel, the job. What a matrix multiply computes, how much work it is, what one unit of that work costs a GPU, and the scoreboard the rest of the book climbs.

Problem · C = A × B, 4,092 × 4,092 · 137 GFLOP · the same job for all ten kernels

All ten kernels in this book compute the same thing. The first one gets 1.3% of the machine and the last one gets 93.7%, and if you ran them side by side on the same two matrices you couldn't tell the outputs apart. Nothing in the ladder changes the arithmetic. What changes is how the numbers get to the arithmetic.

So let's be exact about the job before we start rewriting it. There is no CUDA in this chapter. It covers what a matrix multiply computes, how many operations that comes to, what one of those operations costs on this particular card, and the chart we are trying to climb. If you already know that a 4,092 × 4,092 multiply is 137 GFLOP and that an A6000 peaks somewhere around 30 TFLOP/s, skip to chapter 2.

1.1The names on the box

The routine has a name, and the name is SGEMM: Single-precision GEneral Matrix Multiply. Single precision means 32-bit floats. General means the routine assumes nothing about the contents of the matrices — no symmetry, no band structure, no zeros it is allowed to skip. Every element gets multiplied by every element it should be multiplied by.

The GEMM part comes from BLAS, the Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms. BLAS is a catalogue of routines agreed in the 1970s that every serious numerical library implements: dot products, matrix-vector products, matrix-matrix products, each with a fixed name and a fixed signature. The catalogue is why the same call can be answered by a different, faster library on a different machine without anybody rewriting the caller.

cuBLAS is NVIDIA's implementation of BLAS for its own GPUs. It ships with CUDA, it is closed source, and when PyTorch multiplies two matrices, this is very often the code that actually runs. It matters to us for one reason: it is the competitor. Every percentage in this book is a percentage of cuBLAS on the same problem and the same card, which makes cuBLAS 100% by definition. It's not a technique we are going to learn. It's the professionally tuned thing we are trying to catch, and in the last chapter we'll look at why the gap doesn't quite close.

One footnote on the name before we drop it. GEMM does not compute C = AB. It computes C = αAB + βC, where α and β are scalars, so a caller can scale the product and accumulate it into an existing C. Those two scalars add two multiplies per output element and change nothing else in anything that follows, so this book treats the operation as C = AB and leaves them out.

1.2One cell of C

Here is the definition, and it is the whole operation:

C[i][j] = A[i][0]·B[0][j] + A[i][1]·B[1][j] + … + A[i][K-1]·B[K-1][j]

// written with a sum:  C[i][j] = Σ over k of  A[i][k] · B[k][j]

Every element of C is the dot product of one row of A with one column of B. Take the top-left cell of a small example. Row 0 of A is 9, 6, 7, 9 and column 0 of B is 9, 7, 5, 7. Pair them off in order, multiply each pair, add the four products: 81 + 42 + 35 + 63 = 221. That is C[0][0]. Every other cell is the identical procedure with its own row and its own column.

Two things fall out of that picture. The first is a count: four multiplies and four adds for one cell. With rows of length K it is K multiplies and K adds, or 2K operations per output element. The 4 × 4 example has 16 cells at 8 operations each, which is 128 operations for the whole multiply, and 128 happens to be 2 × 4³.

The second is about reuse, and it will matter for the next ten chapters. Row 0 of A was read to produce C[0][0], and it gets read again for C[0][1], C[0][2] and C[0][3] — every cell in row 0 of C wants the same row of A. Column 0 of B is likewise wanted by every cell in column 0 of C. In a 4,092 × 4,092 multiply, each row of A is needed 4,092 times over. Whether it is fetched 4,092 times is entirely up to the kernel, and that single question is most of what separates 1.3% from 93.7%.

1.3How big the job is

For square matrices of side N the multiply costs 2N³ floating-point operations: N² cells, each a dot product of length N, each dot product 2N operations. Cubic growth is easy to underestimate. Double the side and the work goes up eightfold, not twofold — the matrix gets four times as many cells and each cell gets twice as much work.

Simon Boehm's benchmark, which this book follows, uses N = 4,092. That is deliberately not a power of two. So:

  • C has 4,092² = 16,744,464 elements, roughly 16.7 million.
  • Each element needs 4,092 multiplies and 4,092 adds, which is 8,184 operations.
  • The whole multiply is 2 × 4,092³ = 137,036,693,376 operations.

That's 137 GFLOP, and it's the number in the title of this chapter. It's also the same 137 GFLOP for every kernel in the book. None of the ten does fewer operations than the others; there is no clever reformulation hiding in here that skips work. When kernel 10 finishes seventy times faster than kernel 1, it has done exactly as many multiplies and exactly as many adds.

1.4What a FLOP is, and what the machine can do

A FLOP is one floating-point operation: one multiply, or one add. Counting them is how we hold a job up against a machine, because a machine's headline speed is quoted in FLOPs per second.

Aside · FLOP and FLOP/s

The two are easy to mix up because they're one letter apart. A FLOP is a count of operations, so the job here is 137 GFLOP — a fixed amount of work, like a distance. A FLOP/s is a rate, so the card is 30 TFLOP/s — a speed. Divide the count by the rate and you get a time, which is the only reason either number is interesting.

GPUs don't issue a separate multiply and a separate add for a dot product. They have a fused multiply-add, an FMA, which computes a × b + c in one step. It is one instruction, it counts as two FLOPs, and one FP32 lane retires one FMA per clock cycle. That last sentence is where the machine's peak number comes from, and you can build the number by hand.

The card in this book is an NVIDIA RTX A6000, an Ampere part. It has 84 streaming multiprocessors, usually written SMs. Each SM has 128 FP32 lanes. That's 84 × 128 = 10,752 lanes, and the base clock is 1.41 GHz. Multiply the three together, remembering that each lane's FMA is worth two FLOPs:

NVIDIA's datasheet says 38.7 TFLOP/s, and both figures are correct: the datasheet uses the 1.80 GHz boost clock and this one uses the 1.41 GHz base clock. Boehm works with 30 TFLOP/s throughout, and so does this book.

Now the division that sets up everything else. 137,036,693,376 FLOPs divided by 30 × 10¹² FLOPs per second is 4.6 milliseconds. That's the floor. If all 10,752 lanes did useful arithmetic on every cycle from the first to the last, the multiply would take 4.6 ms, and no kernel — not ours, not cuBLAS, not anything — can go below it.

Kernel 1 takes about 445 ms. cuBLAS takes 5.9.

1.5The scoreboard

Here is the whole book on one chart. Eight kernels rather than ten: Boehm's kernels 7 and 8 were experiments with shared-memory bank conflicts, they came out slower, and he skips them in his own writeup, so we skip them too. Everything is measured on the same 4,092 × 4,092 multiply on the same A6000.

Two things to notice before we start climbing it.

The first is where the speed actually comes from. Kernels 1 through 5 take the throughput from 309 to 15,972 GFLOP/s, a factor of 52, and every one of those steps is about memory: reading it in the right order, keeping a copy of it somewhere close, and making each thread do more arithmetic per byte it fetches. Boehm spent two weekends on kernels 1 to 6 and got to 80% of cuBLAS. The next four weekends went on autotuning and warptiling, and bought the remaining fourteen points. Keep the shape of that curve in mind when you reach the last three bars — most of the win is early and cheap, and the rest is late and expensive.

The second is that even cuBLAS leaves a fifth of the chip unused: 23,250 GFLOP/s against a peak of 30,000, which is about 78%. Nobody reaches the right-hand edge of that chart. Chapter 14 gets into why.

The framing sentence

Every kernel does the same 137 GFLOP. What changes from one kernel to the next is how many bytes must move to feed that arithmetic, and how many instructions get spent moving them.

Which leaves the question the rest of the book is an answer to, and it is a subtraction. The arithmetic units on this card could finish the multiply in 4.6 ms. The obvious kernel takes 445. Where do the other 440 milliseconds go? Not into arithmetic, because every kernel does the same 137 GFLOP. They go into waiting — for bytes that arrive in the wrong shape, or in the wrong order, or that were already fetched thirty-one times before by somebody else.

Chapter 2 puts a number on how much of that waiting the memory system can honestly account for. It's a smaller number than you would expect.
