Chapter 10 · The instruction-starved kernels

Occupancy: the packing game, and why it is not the goal

Kernel 5 has lower occupancy than kernel 3 and runs five times faster. Threads, registers and shared memory are three budgets on every SM, and what you buy with them matters more than how full they are.

Concept · resident warps per SM · kernel 3 at 66%, kernel 5 near 50% · why the slower kernel has the higher number

Kernel 5 asks each thread to hold 64 running totals, plus its eight A operands, plus its eight B operands, plus its indices. Those 64 accumulators must live in registers for the whole kernel; that is the entire point of them. So the thread needs at least 64 + 8 + 8 = 80 registers, and in practice a little more. Registers are a fixed, shared resource on the SM, so this is not free. To see what it costs, we need the packing game from the first post, now played with two real kernels.

10.1What occupancy means

An SM on this GPU can host at most 1,536 threads at once, which is 48 warps. Warps that are loaded onto the SM are called resident, and occupancy is simply the fraction of the 48 slots that are filled. It matters for the reason chapter 3 gave: when a warp stalls waiting for memory, the scheduler switches to another resident warp that is ready. Resident warps are the raw material of latency hiding. Fewer of them, and a stall is more likely to leave a scheduler with nothing to issue.

How many warps are resident is decided by how many blocks fit on the SM, and a block fits only if it fits three budgets at once: threads, registers, and shared memory. The SM accepts blocks until one of the three runs out, and whichever runs out first decides.

10.2How many blocks fit: kernel 3

For kernel 3 the numbers are exactly known from Boehm's profiler. Each block is 1,024 threads. The compiler assigned 37 registers per thread, which the hardware rounds up to 40 because registers are handed out to a warp in units of 256: 1,280 per warp, 40,960 per block. And the block declares 8 KB of shared memory, to which the runtime adds 1 KB of its own bookkeeping: 9,216 bytes.

The smallest wins: one block, which is 1,024 threads, which is 32 warps of the 48 available. Shared memory was never close to being the constraint; threads and registers were, together. Sixty-six percent sounds like the kind of number that explains a slow kernel. Hold that thought.

10.3Kernel 5 in the same game

Kernel 5's block is 256 threads, eight warps, and each thread wants at least 80 registers. Suppose the compiler assigns exactly 80: that is 2,560 per warp and 20,480 per block. Shared memory is 8 KB plus the 1 KB overhead again, 9,216 bytes. Play the same game.

So here is the uncomfortable fact. Kernel 5 has lower occupancy than kernel 3, and is 5.4× faster: 15,972 against 2,980 GFLOP/s. If occupancy were the thing to maximize, that could not happen. So it is not the thing to maximize, and it is worth being precise about why.

10.4Two ways to hide latency

Latency hiding can come from two different places. The first is what chapter 3 described: many warps, so that when one stalls the scheduler switches to another. Call it thread-level parallelism. The second is many independent instructions inside a single warp, so that while one multiply-add is still in flight the next can already be issued, because it does not depend on the first. Call it instruction-level parallelism.

Kernel 3 has essentially none of the second kind. Its inner loop has one accumulator, and each iteration must wait for the previous one, so its only defence against latency is having lots of warps. Kernel 5 has 64 accumulators, and the 64 multiply-adds in its inner loop are completely independent of one another. The scheduler can keep many of them in flight from a single warp. It buys with instruction-level parallelism what kernel 3 had to buy with warps, and it gets a much better deal, because those same registers also eliminated seven eighths of the shared-memory traffic.

Aside · the practical rule

Occupancy is a means, not an end. What you actually want is for the arithmetic units to never run dry, and warps are only one way to arrange that. Chasing 100% occupancy by shrinking the per-thread tile would hand back the register-level caching that made kernel 5 fast in the first place. The useful question is never "is my occupancy high?" but "is anything starving?", and chapter 7 showed how to find out.

10.5Why not 16 × 16

This also answers the question kernel 5 left hanging. If an 8 × 8 thread tile is good, why not 16 × 16, with 256 accumulators? Because 256 accumulators alone are more than the 255 registers a thread may have at all, before counting the operands and indices, and long before that limit the register file would allow so few resident warps that nothing could hide even the shared-memory latency. Going the other way, a 4 × 4 tile needs only about 24 registers and would let the SM fill every warp slot, but it spends 0.5 loads per multiply-add, twice kernel 5's, and chapter 7 said that ratio is what binds.

The 8 × 8 tile sits near the sweet spot of that trade, and finding sweet spots like it is precisely the kind of thing an autotuner is good at, as chapter 12 will show. Before that, chapter 11 picks up the smaller gains that are left once the traffic problems are solved: it starts shaving instructions.
