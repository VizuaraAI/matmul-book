Chapter 2 · Foundations

Two clocks: compute-bound or memory-bound

A kernel waits on the arithmetic or it waits on the bytes. For this multiply the answer should be the arithmetic, by a factor of thirteen — and the first kernel manages to get the other answer.

Two limits · 30 TFLOP/s of arithmetic, 768 GB/s of memory · which one a kernel hits decides everything

Chapter 1 ended on a subtraction. The FP32 units on this card could finish the multiply in 4.6 milliseconds, and the obvious kernel takes 445. Four hundred and forty milliseconds go somewhere, and not into arithmetic, because every kernel in the book does the same 137 GFLOP.

They go into waiting. The useful thing about a GPU kernel is that there are only two things it can wait for: the lanes that do the multiplying, and the bytes that feed them. Whichever of those runs out first sets the runtime, and the other one has slack. So the job of this chapter is to time the multiply twice — once against the arithmetic, once against the memory — and see which clock is the slow one. Both numbers are already on the table from chapter 1.

2.1The arithmetic clock

The first clock we've met. The multiply is 137,036,693,376 floating-point operations, the card retires about 30 trillion of them per second, and dividing one by the other gives 4.6 ms.

What makes that number worth writing down is that it doesn't depend on any decision a kernel author gets to make. The 137 GFLOP is fixed by the problem: 4,092² output cells, each a dot product of length 4,092, no shortcuts available in a general matrix multiply. The 30 TFLOP/s is fixed by the silicon: 10,752 lanes, one fused multiply-add each per cycle, 1.41 GHz. Their ratio is a floor. Every kernel in this book is above it, cuBLAS included, and nothing anyone writes will go below it.

Call that the arithmetic clock. It reads 4.6 ms.

2.2The memory clock

The second clock counts bytes instead of operations. It asks a different question: how long would this multiply take if the arithmetic were free and the only cost were moving the data?

A, B and C are each 4,092 × 4,092 single-precision floats, four bytes apiece, which is about 67 MB per matrix. Now ask what has to cross between the card's memory and its SMs, at an absolute minimum, for the multiply to be possible at all. A has to be read: every element of it is needed. B has to be read. C has to be written, and in a full GEMM it also has to be read, because the routine accumulates into it — that's the βC term chapter 1 set aside. Four passes over 67 MB comes to about 268 MB.

At 768 GB/s, 268 MB takes 0.35 ms.

I should be honest about what that number is. It's a lower bound in the same sense as the arithmetic floor, not a prediction: it assumes each byte crosses the bus exactly once, which requires that the kernel never has to go back for a value it already had. No kernel achieves it — kernel 1 misses it by a factor of two thousand, and even kernel 5, seven chapters of work later, is still sixteen times above it. But nothing beats it either, because the data does have to arrive.

2.3Compute-bound, memory-bound, and the roofline

Two clocks, 4.6 ms and 0.35 ms. A kernel isn't finished until both have finished, so the slower one is the runtime and the faster one has time to spare. Here the arithmetic clock is slower by 4.6 ÷ 0.35, which is 13.

That is the whole diagnosis for an ideal matmul, and it's good news. A perfect kernel on this problem spends its time waiting on the FP32 units, with the memory system idle for something like 92% of the run. The problem has enough arithmetic in it to keep the machine busy thirteen times over. Matmul is, by nature, compute-bound — a job whose limit is the arithmetic. Its opposite is a memory-bound job, one whose limit is the traffic, which is where you land when there isn't enough arithmetic to do per byte fetched. Adding two vectors is the classic case: two loads and a store for one add, and no amount of clever scheduling saves it.

The quantity that decides which side you're on has a name: arithmetic intensity. It's the number of FLOPs you get to do for each byte you had to move from memory, and it's a property of the algorithm as written, not of the hardware. The hardware contributes one number of its own, its balance point — how many FLOPs it can do in the time it takes to move one byte:

// the machine's balance point
30 TFLOP/s  ÷  768 GB/s   =   39 FLOPs per byte

// what you can actually reach, given an intensity I
attainable = min( 30 TFLOP/s ,  768 GB/s × I )

Thirty-nine is the number to remember. A kernel that performs more than 39 FLOPs per byte it fetches is asking more of the arithmetic than of the memory, so it's compute-bound and its ceiling is 30 TFLOP/s. A kernel below 39 is memory-bound, and its ceiling is bandwidth times intensity — for every byte per second the machine can deliver, it gets I FLOPs done. Plot both ceilings against intensity and you get the roofline: a sloped roof on the left where bytes are the limit, a flat roof on the right where the lanes are, and a ridge at 39 where they cross.

Our multiply's own intensity is 137 GFLOP over 268 MB, which is 511 FLOPs per byte. That sits far to the right of the ridge, on the flat roof, at 30 TFLOP/s — the same statement as "compute-bound by 13×", drawn instead of divided.

Aside · Where the picture comes from

The roofline is the central idea of the JAX Scaling Book, and the version drawn here is a simplified one: a single chip, a single kernel, one bandwidth number and one FLOP/s number. It's honest as far as it goes. What it leaves out is mostly the caches, which is exactly where the next section runs into trouble.

2.4What the naive kernel really moves

So a matmul should be compute-bound with room to spare. Kernel 1 gets 309 GFLOP/s out of 30,000. Something has clearly gone wrong, and since the arithmetic is fixed, the only thing left to get wrong is the traffic.

Kernel 1 gives one thread to each of the 16,744,464 cells of C, and each thread runs a loop over k, and each pass through the loop loads one element of A and one element of B. That is 2 × 4,092 = 8,184 loads per thread. Across all the threads:

  • 16,744,464 threads × 8,184 loads = 137,036,693,376 loads.
  • Each load is 4 bytes, so the kernel asks the memory system for about 548 GB.

The minimum was 268 MB. This is 2,046 times that. And the load count is worth a second look: 137,036,693,376 is the FLOP count as well, because the loop body does two loads and two FLOPs. Kernel 1 requests one 4-byte value from memory for every single floating-point operation it performs.

Run that through the roofline. Kernel 1's intensity is 137 GFLOP ÷ 548 GB = 0.25 FLOPs per byte, against the 39 the machine wants — more than two decades to the left of the ridge, deep in memory-bound territory. The roof at that intensity is 768 GB/s × 0.25 = 192 GFLOP/s. A compute-bound problem has been turned into a memory-bound kernel by nothing but the order the values are asked for.

The measurement is 309 GFLOP/s, which is above the roof, and that gap is instructive rather than embarrassing. The 548 GB is what the SMs request. It isn't what the DRAM delivers, because a request that hits in the 6 MB L2 cache is served there and never reaches memory. Neighboring threads in kernel 1 want overlapping data, enough of it arrives close enough together to still be resident, and the cache quietly absorbs a share of the repeats. That's why the kernel does 1.6× better than the simple roofline says.

That gives us a rule for the rest of the book, in its honest form: caches soften the penalty for asking twice, they don't remove it. Every kernel after this one either asks fewer times or asks in a better shape, and not one of them gets to skip that work by hoping the cache will cover it. Chapter 4 takes kernel 1 apart load by load and watches those bytes move.

2.5The one sentence this book is about

Everything in the ten kernels ahead is a variation on one move, and the two clocks are the reason it works. The arithmetic clock can't be improved, so it is not a target. The memory clock is the one every optimization goes after — not by moving bytes faster, which is fixed at 768 GB/s, but by needing fewer of them for the same 137 GFLOP.

The sentence

Every kernel from here on does the same 137 GFLOP of arithmetic. What changes, every single time, is how many bytes have to move to feed it, and how many instructions get spent moving them. Performance is the ratio between arithmetic and traffic, and each optimization raises that ratio.

Here is that claim as a chart, before we've earned any of it. The bar across the top is the arithmetic, and it is the same bar in every frame. The ones underneath are the requests each kernel makes of the memory system, and they are what the next seven chapters are for.

Two footnotes on that chart, because it can be read too literally. The bars count requests, not DRAM traffic — the L2 cache stands between the two and serves a good share of them, so the memory bus always sees less than what is drawn. And the last step doesn't get you to the floor: kernel 5 still asks for 4.3 GB against a minimum of 268 MB, sixteen times too much, and yet by then the traffic has stopped being the thing holding it back. Something else has taken over as the limit, which is a surprising enough turn that chapter 7 is about nothing else.

Next, though, the machine itself: threads, warps, the three memories a kernel can put a value in, and the 32-byte unit that memory actually moves. Chapter 3 lays out the parts. Chapter 4 uses them to write the slowest kernel in the book.
