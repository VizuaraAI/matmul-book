Chapter 12 · The last mile

Kernel 9: autotuning (and the bank conflicts of kernels 7 and 8)

Five tile parameters, a few hundred legal shapes, one winner per chip. First, though, a detour through the two kernels Boehm wrote and threw away, because what they taught is worth more than what they cost.

Kernel · sgemmAutotuned · 19,721 GFLOP/s · 84.8% of cuBLAS · measured by Simon Boehm on an NVIDIA A6000

Between kernel 6 and the autotuned kernel 9 there are two kernels Boehm skips in his own write-up. He wrote them while working out how to remove shared-memory bank conflicts from the vectorized kernel, they did remove the conflicts, and they were slower overall. I'm not going to skip them entirely, for two reasons. Bank conflicts are the last piece of shared-memory mechanics this book hasn't shown, and the lesson of kernels 7 and 8 is one of the most useful in the whole worklog: removing a stall is not the same as making a kernel faster, if the stall you removed wasn't the one that was binding.

12.1What a bank conflict is

Chapter 7 drew shared memory as 32 banks, each able to hand out one 4-byte word per cycle, with consecutive words in consecutive banks: word w lives in bank w mod 32. A warp's load is served in one cycle when its 32 addresses land in 32 different banks, or when several threads want the same word (that is a broadcast, and it is free). The bad case is the third one: two or more threads wanting different words that live in the same bank. The bank can only produce one word per cycle, so the requests queue. An n-way conflict takes n cycles instead of one.

The classic way to get one is to read down a column of a row-major tile whose row length is a multiple of 32. Thread t reads word 32t: every address is a multiple of 32, so every one of them is in bank 0, and a single load instruction takes 32 cycles. The classic fix is just as blunt: make the rows 33 words long instead of 32. Now thread t reads word 33t, which lands in bank t, and the same load takes one cycle. That padding trick is exactly what Boehm's kernel 8 does; its name in his repository is sgemmResolveBankExtraCol.

Kernel 6's shared-memory loads have a milder version of this: the 128-bit loads of Bs that different threads of a warp issue are spaced 32 bytes apart, so some of them collide in the banks and are served in more than one pass. Kernels 7 and 8 rearranged the tiles to make those accesses conflict-free, and the profiler confirmed the conflicts were gone. The kernels were still slower. The extra index arithmetic and the less regular layout cost more instructions than the conflicts had cost cycles, and instructions were the binding resource by then. Boehm kept the diagnosis and dropped the kernels, which is the right call and a good habit: measure the whole thing, not the stall you were staring at.

12.2Five parameters, no theory

By now the kernel has five free parameters. The block tile is BM × BN. The chunk width is BK. The thread tile is TM × TN. Together they fix the number of threads per block, BM × BN ÷ (TM × TN), how much shared memory a chunk needs, how many registers each thread holds, and how many blocks can be resident on an SM. Every choice trades against the others. Bigger tiles mean more reuse but more registers and more shared memory; more threads mean better latency hiding but a smaller share of the register file each; a wider BK means fewer barriers per chunk but a bigger tile to hold.

Nobody can reason their way to the optimum, because it depends on the specific SM's register file, its shared-memory size, its scheduler count, the L2, and the matrix shape. So Boehm stopped reasoning and started measuring: generate every legal combination, compile each one, run it, keep the winner. That is autotuning, and every high-performance library does it.

12.3Filter, then measure

Not every combination is legal, and the hardware's own rules do most of the pruning before anything is timed. The thread count has to be a multiple of 32 and at most 1,024. The two tiles of a chunk have to fit in 48 KB of shared memory. Each thread's accumulators and operands have to fit in 255 registers. And the cooperative load has to divide evenly: with 128-bit loads, each thread copies four floats at a time, so BM × BK and BN × BK must both be multiples of four times the thread count, and Boehm's kernel adds a few more divisibility conditions so that no thread ever handles a partial row. The animation applies those rules to a grid of candidates, live, and counts what survives.

On the A6000 the winner was BM = BN = 128, BK = 16, TM = TN = 8: the same tiles as kernel 6 with a chunk twice as wide, which means half as many barriers per output and half as many trips through the outer loop. That bought 18,237 → 19,721 GFLOP/s, about 8%. Boehm is candid that he cannot explain why this exact combination wins, and his verdict on the method is worth keeping: "Autotuning works, every high-performance library uses it, but it also feels very unsatisfying."

12.4The optimum belongs to the chip

The lesson is not "tuning gives 8%". It is what happened when he ran the same search on a different card. On an A100, the A6000's winning shape reached 12.0 TFLOP/s, and a completely different shape, BM = BN = 64, BK = 16, TM = TN = 4, reached 12.6. Smaller tiles everywhere, and 5% faster than the shape that was optimal one chip over. The optimum is a property of the specific chip, not of the algorithm.

What autotuning taught

Once a kernel is close to the arithmetic limit, the last few percent depend on details no model captures, and those details differ from chip to chip. So a library that has to run well everywhere cannot ship one tuned kernel. It ships many, and picks between them at runtime. Hold that thought for cuBLAS in chapter 14.

There is one more structural idea left before that, and it comes from the hardware rather than from a search. The tiling so far has two levels, the block and the thread. The machine has a third level in between, the warp, and kernel 10 makes it explicit.

The bank model is Ampere's (32 banks, 4-byte words, one word per bank per cycle).
